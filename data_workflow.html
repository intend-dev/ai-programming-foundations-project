AI Programming Foundations: Titanic Data Workflow¶

Author: Francisco Mateus

Dataset: Titanic: Machine Learning from Disaster (Kaggle)

This notebook builds a reproducible workflow over the 891-row Titanic passenger table. It measures what is missing, cleans the table with four pure functions, explores survival by sex, class, title and deck, draws five labelled figures, and closes with a summary.

How this notebook is organised¶

The headings follow the project's workflow, with Assessment and Cleaning as separate sections:

  1. Setup: the libraries and the versions this run used.
  2. Ingestion: the CSV loaded from disk, with its provenance.
  3. Assessment: what is missing, and where the gaps sit.
  4. Cleaning: the functions that repair it, each one pure.
  5. Exploratory Analysis: survival rates by sex, class, title and deck.
  6. Visualizations: the labelled figures, each interpreted in the cell below it.
  7. Summary: what the numbers support and what they do not.

Every number quoted in the written report is printed by a cell in this notebook.

What the assessment found¶

The findings below set up the cleaning section:

  • Age is blank in 177 rows (19.87% of the table). That is too many to drop, so the blanks need filling.
  • Cabin is blank in 687 rows (77.10%). The column is mostly absent, so the part that still carries information is the deck letter.
  • Embarked is blank in 2 rows. Both rows share ticket 113572 and cabin B28, so one booking produced both gaps and one fill can fix both.
  • Fare is 0 in 15 rows. They look like real records, so I left them as they are.

The other checks found nothing wrong: no duplicate rows, no repeated PassengerId, plausible Age and Fare ranges, and only the values the column dictionary lists.

4. Cleaning¶

In this section I repaired the three gaps the assessment found and derived two new columns, with four pure functions. A pure function copies the frame, changes the copy, and returns it, so nothing outside it changes when it runs.

title comes first, because the age fill reads it.

  1. title_from_name: extracts the honorific from Name.
  2. fill_age: fills the 177 blanks with the median of each title group.
  3. deck_from_cabin: turns Cabin into the deck letter, U when unknown.
  4. fill_embarked: fills the 2 blanks of the shared booking with S.
In [6]:
# Four pure functions: each copies the frame
def title_from_name(df):
    """Extract the honorific, e.g. "Mr", from Name, into a new title column."""
    df = df.copy()
    df["title"] = df["Name"].str.extract(r", ((?:the )?[A-Za-z]+)\.", expand=False)
    # The one peerage name ("Rothes, the Countess.") captures as "the Countess"; strip the leading "the"
    df["title"] = df["title"].str.removeprefix("the ")
    return df


def fill_age(df):
    """Fill Age blanks with each title group's median; the overall median is the fallback."""
    # `Age` blanks take the median of their title group; the overall median is a fallback for a group with no known age (unused here)
    df = df.copy()
    group_median = df.groupby("title")["Age"].transform("median")
    df["Age"] = df["Age"].fillna(group_median)
    df["Age"] = df["Age"].fillna(df["Age"].median())
    return df


def deck_from_cabin(df):
    """Turn Cabin into the deck letter; U when there is no cabin record."""
    df = df.copy()
    df["deck"] = df["Cabin"].str[0]
    df["deck"] = df["deck"].fillna("U")
    return df


def fill_embarked(df):
    """Fill the two blank Embarked values with S, the most common port."""
    # The two blank rows are one booking (ticket `113572`)
    df = df.copy()
    df["Embarked"] = df["Embarked"].fillna("S")
    return df

The four functions are applied in order, and the checks below assert that every gap is closed: 177 age values filled, 2 ports filled, and the deck and title columns populated for all 891 rows.

# Survival rate
def rate_table(df, col):
    """Return n, survived and the survival rate per group of a column."""
    # nothing is assigned into df
    rows = df.groupby(col)["Survived"].agg(n="size", survived="sum")
    rows["rate"] = (rows["survived"] / rows["n"] * 100).round(2)
    return rows

The overall picture¶

I began with the survival rate for the full passenger list, then split it by sex, class, title, and deck. Titles held by 2 passengers or fewer went into a single Rare row, since a rate drawn from that small a sample is mostly noise. The underlying title column kept all 17 values.

38.38 percent of the 891 passengers survived. Of the two primary comparisons, sex and class, sex creates the wider gap. Women survived at 74.20 percent against 18.89 percent for men. Class then moves in clear steps, from 62.96 percent in first class to 47.28 in second and 24.24 in third.

7. Summary¶

Here I will present a summary of what the numbers support, what they rest on, and what they cannot say.
Every claim mentioned here quotes a number a cell or a figure printed above.

Findings summary¶

  1. Fewer than four in ten survived. Of the 891 passengers, 342 survived, 38.38 percent. (Visualization 1.)
  2. Survival differs substantially by sex. Women survived at 74.20 percent against 18.89 for men. (Visualization 2.)
  3. Class orders survival in ticket order. The rate falls step by step from 62.96 percent in first class to 47.28 in second and 24.24 in third. (Visualization 3.)
  4. Title combines sex and life stage. Adult women (Mrs 79.20, Miss 69.78) survive far above the overall line, boys (Master 57.50) survive well above the adult men's rate, and adult men (Mr 15.67) sit far below it. (Visualization 4.)
  5. The cabin record separates the decks. D, E and B sit far above the overall rate, while U, the label for the 687 passengers with no cabin record, falls below it at 29.99. (Visualization 5.)

Note: All findings are associations, not causal effects (see Limitations).

Reflection¶

The title column nearly lost its rarest passenger. My first title regex read Rothes, the Countess. as no title at all, because the follows the comma. The final pattern matches an optional the and strips it, so all 17 honorifics survive the extraction.

Rare is a bucket, not a finding. 11 titles hold 14 passengers between them, so their 64.29 percent rate averages over unrelated people; the label collects titles held by 2 or fewer passengers, and groups under 20 read as weak evidence.

Assumptions¶

  • Survived: the rate is the mean of a 0/1 column, so it compares only next to its group size; every table and figure states n.
  • Age: 177 passengers had no recorded age, and each was filled with their title's median (the overall-median fallback filled nothing).
  • Embarked: the 2 passengers with no recorded port are filled with S, the most common port.
  • deck: passengers without a cabin record carry U instead of being dropped.
  • title: the Rare grouping is display-only; the underlying column keeps all 17 values, and no cell assigns into the loaded frame.

Limitations¶

  • Association, not cause: the data records who was aboard, meaning it is not a designed sample: one voyage, one ship, 891 passengers.
  • Cabin records: Cabin is missing for 687 passengers, so the deck column is mostly U, and the deck findings rest on the 204 passengers with a recorded cabin.
  • Imputed ages: 177 ages are filled at their title's median value, which bunches those passengers onto their group's median instead of their true spread.
  • Small groups: groups under 20 passengers (Rare, Dr and Rev among titles; F, G, A and T among decks) are shown but read as weak evidence.

Possible additional research¶

  • Stratify the deck rates by class to test whether the cabin record carries anything beyond class; this report reads each deck whole, so class and deck remain entangled.
  • Combine SibSp and Parch into a family-size variable and test whether traveling with family associates with survival; neither column is used in this report.
  • Analyze Fare, which is loaded and unused, to test whether cost, through class or cabin, tracks survival beyond the class steps already shown.
  • Add crew lists from other records to test whether "women and children first" extended past the passenger class.
